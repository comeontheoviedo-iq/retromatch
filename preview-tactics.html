    Mark him closely

    A match-day instruction. The side shadows the other lot's danger man, but only if the week's training has already been spent on him. Without that groundwork the lads do not know who they are watching, and pulling a man out of his own game does more harm than good.

    A period-typical diary drawn from cited habits, not a claim that every First Division club kept this exact timetable. Liverpool's work at Melwood was famously built around five-a-sides and pass-and-move. Graham Taylor's Watford rehearsed their pattern of play week in, week out, along with set pieces. Fitness running remained ordinary English midweek work at many other clubs. Individual sides varied.

    Familiarity and form

    Half of a season's trained gain wears off over the summer if the method is dropped. The rest carries into the next campaign. A new shape still costs a run of matches while the side learns it.

    Set pieces

    Corners, free-kicks and long throws are midweek work, not a separate department. Taylor's Watford treated set pieces as part of the weekly pattern. A week spent on them is meant to sharpen those restarts a little, never to invent a new kind of dead-ball specialist.
