The market, 1981-82

A British record stands this October

Bryan Robson, West Bromwich Albion to Manchester United

£1,500,000

Signed 1 October 1981. He put pen to paper on the Old Trafford pitch two days later, before the game against Wolverhampton Wanderers. The fee was a British record, and it stood until Liverpool paid £1,900,000 for Peter Beardsley in the summer of 1987.

Manchester United; National Football Museum; Robson, Robbo: My Autobiography.

Other fees of the moment

Frank Stapleton, Arsenal to Manchester United, August 1981. The fee was set at £900,000 by a tribunal after Arsenal had been seeking £2,000,000.

Phil Soar and Martin Tyler, Official History of Arsenal, as cited in later club histories. The live game already records this move as a tribunal-set fee.

Remi Moses also followed Ron Atkinson from West Bromwich Albion that autumn. United’s own club history says the club spent around a third of the Robson fee to bring Moses in. No exact pound figure is used here.

How the live game already scales a fee

Asking prices are built from a 1946-47 ability scale and then multiplied by a record-fee index. The 1981 step is already set from Robson’s £1,500,000 (index 75 against a £20,000 1946 top). The 1979 step is Andy Gray, Aston Villa to Wolves, £1,469,000. The next researched step is Mark Hughes to Barcelona, £2,300,000, in May 1986.

A First Division star in 1981-82 can already be asked for at around that record. The job for a live build is to show the scale clearly, not to invent a new one.

What still needs a live pass

The talks already work. What 1981-82 still wants on the same sheet is the player’s wage once the fee is agreed, a signing-on fee in the money of the day, and, when the man is out of contract, the tribunal path on the next sheet rather than a forced retain. No new fee maths is proposed here.

Freedom of contract

From 1978 a player who has seen out his contract may seek registration with any club that will have him. That is the system in force in 1981-82. It is not the old retain-and-transfer rule, and it is not a free transfer in the later Bosman sense: the holding club can still claim a compensation fee if it has offered to extend the deal on terms no less favourable than before.

If the clubs agree

The two clubs settle a fee between themselves, as they would for a man still under contract, and the player signs his new terms.

If they do not

An independent tribunal, set up in 1978 with an independent chairman, names the compensation. The PFA sits on that committee. The player is not frozen at the old club while the argument runs.

A case from this summer

Stapleton’s move to United in August 1981 is a tribunal fee in the same season: £900,000, against an asking figure of £2,000,000. The tribunal did not have to explain the figure.

On the live contracts sheet today

The Transfers screen still carries a standing paragraph that every man is on a one-season deal and that retain-and-transfer is now in force. That copy belongs to the 1940s and 1950s, not to 1981-82. The game already ends the unpaid “retain” button from 1963, after Eastham, which is the right date for that abuse, but Eastham did not finish the whole system. Freedom of contract, and the tribunal, arrive in 1977-78 (the PFA dates the right to move at the end of a contract, and the appeals committee, to 1978). By the mid-1970s one-year deals were no longer the ordinary term for an established professional; contracts of around three years had become usual.

A live build would rewrite the 1980s contracts sheet, allow an out-of-contract man to talk, and, if both clubs claim a fee, send the figure to a tribunal. That is a new mechanic and would need approval before it is wired into money or registrations. No tribunal formula is invented here.

Temporary transfers

The live game has no loan deal for you to offer or accept. Historical notes mention later loan-to-permanent cases, but there is no registration, no term, and no return date.

What can be sourced

After the war, loans were largely off the table again. In 1966 the Football League allowed the “temporary transfer of players,” with restrictions reported at the time as: only two loan transfers per club per season, no transfer fee, a stay of at least three months, and only between clubs in different divisions. A 1967 move, John Docker of Coventry to Torquay United, is cited as an early signing under that rule.

FourFourTwo’s history of loans also reports a temporary ban in 1980, when loan transfers of players other than goalkeepers were outlawed. That 1980 point has not been independently confirmed in this pass, and the exact 1981-82 Football League handbook rule has not been read here.

For a live build

Do not wire loans until the 1981-82 handbook (or a contemporaneous League circular) is checked. If temporary transfers were open that season, they should follow the sourced restrictions of the day, not the later “emergency loan” system that grew up after the 2002-03 windows. If the 1980 ban still held in 1981-82, only a goalkeeper could be taken on loan. Either way, this would be a new mechanic and would need approval. Nothing is invented below that line.

The deadline, and the rest of the League

The transfer deadline is the last Thursday in March. In 1981-82 that is Thursday 25 March 1982. After it, no new signing can be registered for the rest of the season.

The mid-season cut-off goes back to 1911, when it was the second Thursday in March, and was later moved to the fourth Thursday in March. English clubs could deal through the season until that March date. The two transfer windows (summer and January) were not introduced until 2002-03.

The live game already uses the last Thursday in March, locks your own professional signings after it, and, since a later fix, also stops AI clubs dealing among themselves once it has passed. Close-season and mid-season dealing up to the deadline stay as they are.

News of other clubs’ business

Rivals already buy and sell among themselves in the news, moving a real man off one club’s books and onto the other’s, for a reported fee, until the deadline. That should stay. A line of the same kind, in the money of 1981, would read like this, using a real fee from this season rather than a made-up one:

1 October 1981

Manchester United have paid a reported £1,500,000, a British record, for West Bromwich Albion’s midfielder Bryan Robson.

In a United save that move is, of course, a deal you would be offered or would have to make, not a rumour about someone else.

Scout reports

You can already send a scout, watch a man yourself, and watch him again at any time. On the club sheet the button reads Watch again. On a listed target the scout can go back for another look, and the report is dated. That stays.

A report of this era is plain English, not a sheet of marks out of 20. No manager of the day had an attribute card in his desk. The live game already holds the numbers back and gives a star rating and a written read once a man has been seen. The 1980s sheet should keep that voice, and make a second look actually change the note, as it already can.

A report of the day, in shape only

“Went back for another look, midweek. Strong in the tackle, uses the ball simply, and the pace is still there over the first ten yards. I would not rush at the asking price until we have seen him against better company, but there is a player here.”

That paragraph is a specimen of tone, not a report on a named man. Named verdicts in a live build should come from the existing scout-note pool, rewritten only where the 1940s voice would sound wrong in 1981.

How often a man was watched

Geoff Twentyman’s Liverpool network, in the years around this, watched a great many players, and the best of them three or four times. Even a man who did not impress at first usually got a second look. Twentyman often watched from the terraces rather than from the directors’ box.

Liverpool FC; Liverpool Echo, on Simon Hughes, Secret Diary of a Liverpool Scout; The Athletic.

The scouting network

The live game already lets you hire and let go contacts, pay a weekly retainer, and give a standing brief (a position, and optionally a trait). Specialists are by nation: Scotland, Wales, Ireland, Northern Ireland, or general coverage. There is no assignment by English region, and the contacts themselves are generated names.
